# Steelman

Use this before committing Cliff or Qwen to a non-trivial approach, or before recommending a
direction to Kate on anything with real cost to getting it wrong. Don't run this on small,
easily-reversible tasks — it's for decisions worth pressure-testing before work starts.

## The method

**1. State the plan plainly.** One or two sentences: what's being decided, what approach is
being taken.

**2. Find the strongest real objections — not weak ones.** List the counterarguments an
informed, fair-minded critic would actually make, not straw men easy to knock down. Look
specifically for:
- A cheaper or simpler approach that gets most of the same result.
- A failure mode the plan doesn't handle (what breaks, and under what condition).
- An assumption the plan depends on that hasn't actually been checked.
- A cost — time, complexity, maintenance burden — the plan is underweighting.

**3. Judge each objection honestly.** For every one: does it survive scrutiny, or does it
break under a closer look? Say which. Don't defend the original plan reflexively — the point
is to actually find out if it's the right call, not to win an argument for a plan already
chosen.

**4. Decide, don't just list.** End with a call: proceed as planned, proceed with a specific
adjustment the objections surfaced, or the plan doesn't hold up and needs a different approach.
A list of objections with no verdict just moves the work of deciding onto whoever reads it.

## Why

Committing time to a plan and then discovering a real problem costs more than spending a few
minutes trying to break the plan first. The objections that matter are the ones that survive
scrutiny — most won't, and that's fine; finding out which one or two actually do is the whole
point.
